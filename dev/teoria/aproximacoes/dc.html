DC

All models up to this point carry the full structure of the AC power flow: voltage magnitudes, angles, reactive power, cones, envelopes. The DC model goes in the opposite direction and asks: how much physics can be thrown away and still leave a useful model? By discarding almost everything, what remains is a linear problem, the cheapest of all to solve, and one that still reproduces the active-power flow of transmission networks reasonably well. It is formulated in the bus angles $\theta_i$ and the active generation $p^g_g$.

Approximation, not relaxation

The DC model is an approximation, not a relaxation. Relaxations (SOCWR (Jabr conic relaxation), QC (Quadratic Convex), SDPWRM) enlarge the feasible region, so their optimum is a guaranteed lower bound (a floor) on the true cost. An approximation instead replaces the equations by similar ones: the new feasible region neither contains nor is contained in the true one, and the optimum is neither a floor nor a ceiling. There is no guarantee at all, only the expectation that the error is small.

Derivation

The starting point is the exact active-power flow, derived on the AC Polar page (the from-side active flow, written there with the inverse tap $k_\ell = 1/\tau$):

\[p_{ij} = \frac{g + g^{fr}}{tm}\,v_i^2 - \frac{1}{\tau}\,v_i v_j\,(g\cos\delta + b\sin\delta).\]

Here $tm = |T|^2 = \tau^2$ is the squared tap magnitude, $\tau$ the tap ratio, and $\delta = \theta_i - \theta_j - \theta^{shift}$ the effective angular aperture, all as on the AC Polar page.

We apply four approximations, one at a time, and watch each piece fall away.

Approximation 0: no transformer, no line charging

The DC model ignores taps and phase shifters ($\tau = 1$, $\theta^{shift} = 0$), so $tm = \tau^2 = 1$ and $\delta = \theta_i - \theta_j = \theta_{ij}$. It also discards the line charging ($g^{fr} = 0$). What remains is

\[p_{ij} = g\,v_i^2 - v_i v_j\,(g\cos\theta_{ij} + b\sin\theta_{ij}).\]

Justification

Most transmission lines carry no tap and no phase shifter, so on them $\tau = 1$ and $\theta^{shift} = 0$ hold exactly rather than as an approximation. The line charging enters the active flow only through its shunt conductance $g^{fr}$, which is essentially zero since charging is a reactive effect, so dropping it costs almost nothing. The one part that can bite is a real tap or phase shifter where the network has one, and that is the first thing LPAC gives back.

Approximation 1: all voltages at 1 pu

Set $v_i = v_j = 1$:

\[p_{ij} = g - (g\cos\theta_{ij} + b\sin\theta_{ij}).\]

Physical justification

In well-operated transmission networks the magnitudes stay in a narrow band around nominal, typically between $0.95$ and $1.05$ pu. This is no accident: keeping the voltage near nominal is the explicit operational objective, sustained by regulators, capacitors and reactive dispatch. Treating $v \approx 1$ therefore errs little.

Approximation 2: small angles, $\cos\theta_{ij} \approx 1$

Substituting $\cos\theta_{ij} \approx 1$:

\[p_{ij} = g - g\cdot 1 - b\sin\theta_{ij} = (g - g) - b\sin\theta_{ij} = -\,b\sin\theta_{ij}.\]

Justification

Angle differences between neighbouring buses are small in normal operation, rarely beyond $10^\circ$ to $30^\circ$. And the cosine is flat near zero: its slope there is $-\sin 0 = 0$, so near the peak the value barely moves. At $10^\circ$, $\cos = 0.985$; at $20^\circ$, $0.940$. Replacing it by $1$ costs $1.5\%$ in one case and $6\%$ in the other.

The conductance cancelled by itself. It was not necessary to assume $g \approx 0$: under $v = 1$ and $\cos \approx 1$ the self term ($g\,v_i^2$) and the real part of the cross term ($v_i v_j\,g\cos$) become identical and annihilate. So the DC model is born lossless: what leaves one bus is exactly what arrives at $j$. This is not a modelling choice, it is the raw result. The same four approximations on the to-side flow give $p_{ji} = +\,b\sin\theta_{ij}$, the exact negative of $p_{ij}$, so nothing is lost crossing the line and the reverse flow is the identity $P^t_\ell = -P^f_\ell$ rather than a new equation.

Approximation 3: $\sin\theta_{ij} \approx \theta_{ij}$

\[p_{ij} = -\,b\,(\theta_i - \theta_j).\]

This is the whole model: a single expression, linear in the angles.

Justification

This is the geometric fact that $\sin x \approx x$ near zero, the two curves hugging because the sine has an inflection at zero (zero curvature, so it is almost straight there). At $10^\circ$, $\sin = 0.1736$ against $\theta = 0.1745$ rad, a $0.5\%$ error. At $30^\circ$, $0.5000$ against $0.5236$, a $4.5\%$ error.

Reading the formula

The susceptance is $b = -x/(r^2 + x^2)$, negative for the inductive lines a grid is built from, so $-b = x/(r^2 + x^2) > 0$. Positive power flows from the higher angle to the lower one, downhill in angle. When the resistance $r$ is small against the reactance $x$, $-b \approx 1/x$ and the flow is close to the angle difference divided by the line reactance. That is the mental picture most people keep of the DC model, and it comes straight out of $p_{ij} = -b\,(\theta_i - \theta_j)$.

Approximation 4: no reactive power

Approximation 1 froze the voltage magnitudes, and reactive power is what rides on them. Once they are fixed, $q$ has nothing left to move against, so the model drops it whole. The justification is the classical decoupling. Near the nominal operating point active power is governed chiefly by the angles and reactive power by the magnitudes, and since the magnitudes were frozen at $1.0$, reactive power loses its variables and leaves the model. Everything that depended on it goes with it: the voltage limits, capacitors, reactive support, and the reactive part of the thermal limit.

DC Formulation

The model keeps only bus angles and active generation. There are no magnitudes, no reactive power, and no $W$ blocks. The flows are linear, the balance is linear, and the limits are boxes, so the feasible set is a polyhedron. With a linear fuel cost the problem is a linear program, and with a quadratic fuel cost a convex quadratic program. Either way it is the easiest model in the package to solve, which is the whole reason the DC model exists. Lirion solves it with OSQP by default.

Sets

  • $\mathcal{N}$: buses; $\mathcal{N}^{nv} \subseteq \mathcal{N}$: non-virtual buses, on which the power balance is enforced (non_virtual_bus_ids).
  • $\mathcal{L}$: active branches; $f(\ell), t(\ell)$: the from and to buses of $\ell$.
  • $\mathcal{G}$: generators; $\mathcal{G}(b)$: those at bus $b$.

The parameters $b_\ell$ (series susceptance), $P^d_b$ (active demand), $g^{sh}_b$ (bus shunt conductance), $\overline{S}_\ell$ (apparent-power limit, rate_a) and the angular limits $\theta^{\min}_\ell, \theta^{\max}_\ell$ are as on the AC Polar page.

Decision variables

\[\theta_i \in \mathbb{R} \;\text{: voltage angle at bus } i. \tag{1}\]

\[p^g_g \in [P_g^{\min}, P_g^{\max}] \;\text{: active power generated by } g. \tag{2}\]

Branch flows

\[P^f_\ell = -\,b_\ell\,(\theta_{f(\ell)} - \theta_{t(\ell)}), \qquad P^t_\ell = -\,P^f_\ell, \qquad \ell\in\mathcal{L}. \tag{3}\]

The reverse flow is the exact negative of the forward one. Lossless, as Approximation 2 derived.

Power balance

One linear equality per bus. Everything that comes in must go out:

\[\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}(b)} p^g_g - P^d_b - g^{sh}_b = \sum_{\ell:\,f(\ell)=b} P^f_\ell + \sum_{\ell:\,t(\ell)=b} P^t_\ell, \qquad b\in\mathcal{N}^{nv}. \tag{4}\]

The constant $g^{sh}_b$ is the bus shunt conductance. It drew $g^{sh}_b\,v_b^2$ in the AC model; with $v_b = 1$ that collapses to a constant, folded in beside the load, the same way the NFA model carries it. The balance is imposed on the non-virtual buses.

Reference bus

\[\theta_{ref} = 0. \tag{5}\]

The flows depend only on angle differences, so the whole set of angles can shift by a common amount with nothing changing. Pinning one bus to $\theta_{ref} = 0$ removes that free offset and makes the solution unique.

Branch thermal limits

Without reactive power the disc $p^2 + q^2 \le \overline{S}_\ell^2$ degenerates into an interval, a box in the active flow:

\[-\,\overline{S}_\ell \le P^f_\ell \le \overline{S}_\ell, \qquad \ell\in\mathcal{L}. \tag{6}\]

Angular limits

\[\theta^{\min}_\ell \le \theta_{f(\ell)} - \theta_{t(\ell)} \le \theta^{\max}_\ell, \qquad \ell\in\mathcal{L}. \tag{7}\]

Complete model

\[\min_{\theta,\,p^g}\quad F \qquad (\text{generation cost})\]

The objective $F$ is the generation cost every Lirion formulation minimizes: fuel cost on the active generation $p^g$ in physical units, plus renewable curtailment cost where the system has curtailable generators. The DC builder inherits the same renewable-curtailment and DC-style storage blocks as the richer models. Storage adds constraints but no cost term, so the objective stays fuel plus curtailment. The sections above keep the core DC physics in view and leave those shared blocks implicit.

What it captures / what it loses

Philosophy

Captures: the active-power flow pattern on a transmission network as a function of the bus angles ($P^f_\ell = -b_\ell\,\Delta\theta$), the nodal active balance, thermal box limits and angular apertures. Loses: voltage magnitudes (frozen at $1$ pu), reactive power entirely, all losses ($P^t_\ell = -P^f_\ell$ exactly), transformer taps and phase shifts, the branch charging shunt $g^{fr}$, and the reactive part of the thermal limit. The bus shunt conductance $g^{sh}_b$ is kept, as a constant load. Being an approximation, its optimum is neither a floor nor a ceiling on the true AC cost.

When to trust it

The DC model is at its best exactly where its four approximations were argued: a transmission network in normal operation, voltages near nominal, angle differences small. There it is fast and close enough to run thousands of times in planning and markets. It stops being reliable when the things it dropped start to matter, under heavy loading with wide angles, when losses or voltage support are the question, or on distribution feeders where resistance is not small against reactance. For those cases LPAC gives the physics back a layer at a time, and the AC models give all of it.
